The model had to earn the right to trade

Verity prices Bitcoin settlement markets on Kalshi against live Coinbase data, journals every decision it makes, and returns no-trade when the evidence is thin. It ran paper-only for months. Version 4 was the first one to clear the gates I had written for it, so I turned on real capital.

Live · real capital, Kalshi 5 days live at time of writing +$540 realised v1–v3 never got the keys
01The problem

Everything about this invites you to fool yourself

Kalshi settles Bitcoin markets on a specific price at a specific minute. That makes them scoreable in a way most prediction markets are not. You can build an estimate of the settlement probability, compare it against what the market is charging, and check afterwards whether you were right.

Most people who try this lose money. The market is usually closer to correct than a model built on a desktop, and the ways to fool yourself are cheap. A lookahead bug. A timestamp that drifts by a few seconds. A backtest that never had to pay a spread. Every one of those produces a beautiful equity curve and an empty account.

So the design problem was never the model. It was building something that would tell me, out loud and in writing, when the model was wrong.

02How it works

Four gates, in order

01

Bound the inputs

Coinbase supplies public market data. Kalshi supplies market prices. Every number is stamped with the moment it arrived, so a comparison cannot quietly read the future. This is the boring part and it is the part that kills most homemade systems.

02

Price it twice

A settlement-aware estimate sits next to the market midpoint from the same snapshot. Source quality, timing and uncertainty stay visible on the screen instead of collapsing into one confident number.

03

Gate the decision

Missing evidence returns no-trade. Thin expected edge returns no-trade. Unreliable timing returns no-trade. Every rejection lands in the ledger with its reason attached, which is what makes the sample honest when you go back and count.

04

Earn execution

Order entry stayed switched off until a version passed those gates on its own terms, on data it had not been tuned against. v1 through v3 did not. v4 did, and that is the only reason it has money behind it.

03Going live

What changed, and what it is worth so far

Paper mode proved the system could refuse. Going live tested something different: whether the edge survived contact with a spread, a fill, and the seconds between deciding and being filled. Those are the costs a backtest hides.

Where this claim stops

Five days is not a sample. $540 is real money and it is also a small number of decisions. At this count I cannot separate a model that works from a week that went my way, and I am not going to pretend the difference is settled. What would change my mind is a few hundred gated decisions with the no-trade rate holding steady and the realised edge staying inside the range the model predicted for itself. Until then the defensible claim is narrow: the system does what its specification says, it refuses more often than it acts, and it has not lost money doing it.

04Disclosure

Who did what

I owned

Problem framing, settlement-rule and source research, the product requirements, the acceptance criteria, the evaluation gates, what the interface prioritises, and every go / no-go call including the three that said no.

AI implemented

Coding agents generated a large share of the code, tests and documentation from my plans, then revised it through iterative feedback. I reviewed and directed. I did not hand-write most of the implementation and I am not going to claim I did.

Evidence bar

Timestamped inputs, causal timing checks, journaled decisions with reasons, explicit rejection gates, and conclusions allowed to land on “not proven”. Execution stayed disabled until the gates said otherwise.

How I work

AI coding agents are my implementation layer. What I bring is deciding what to build, turning research into constraints precise enough to test, and refusing to call a result successful before the evidence supports it. Three versions of this system got refused by their own gates. That is the part of the process I would want a hiring manager to look at.
